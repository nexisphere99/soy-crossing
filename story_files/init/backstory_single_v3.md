# Shape of You: Manuel's Story   Backstory (REVISED: Single)

---

The thing about getting fired is that your body knows before your brain catches up.

I was sitting in the all-hands at Brevity Labs on a Tuesday morning, coffee going cold in my hand, watching our CEO David stand at the front of the room with that face. You know the face. The one where someone has rehearsed bad news so many times it's gone smooth, like a stone rubbed between anxious fingers. He kept touching the back of his neck. He said "strategic pivot" and "difficult decisions" and "streamlining our operational focus" and my stomach dropped three floors before my brain even started translating the corporate into human.

Six people on my team. All of backend. Gone.

HR walked me to my desk. Fifteen minutes to pack. I had a succulent my mother mailed me when I started (she somehow got a cactus through customs, don't ask), a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST DEVELOPER that my friend Paco gave me as a joke, and a photo of my family in Guadalajara that I kept taped to the monitor. Fifteen minutes was generous. I could have done it in four.

My badge stopped working before I hit the parking lot. I stood there holding my cardboard box in the sunshine like some cliché from a movie about sad white guys in suburbia, except I'm a Mexican dude on an H-1B visa and the sunshine felt like it was mocking me specifically.

I sat in my car for twenty minutes. Called my mother. She didn't pick up. It was Wednesday in Guadalajara, which meant she was at church, which meant God was getting her full attention and I was getting voicemail. I hung up without leaving a message. What would I say? "Hola mamá, I lost the job you sold the car to help me get."

I drove home. Put the box on the kitchen table. Chipi jumped up and knocked the cactus off the edge immediately because that's what Chipi does. I cleaned up the dirt and the broken pot and sat on the floor with my back against the cabinet and that's where the math started.

H-1B visa. Employer-sponsored. No employer, no visa. Sixty-day grace period to find a new sponsor or leave the country.

Leaving means:

Going back to Guadalajara with nothing. Four years of American life dissolved. The apartment I furnished from IKEA drunk by myself. The friends I made. The routines I built. The version of myself that exists here and nowhere else.

Going back to my father Ricardo, retired mechanic, thick hands, who would look at me and see a son who failed at the one thing the family invested in.

Going back to my mother Lupita, who lit candles at church for my success and would now light them for my return, which is a different kind of prayer.

Going back to my brother Héctor, career military, who already thinks I'm soft for choosing computers over something real, and who would be right in the worst possible way.

Sixty days.

I said it out loud to the empty apartment. "Sixty days." Chipi looked at me from the counter where she was already plotting to knock something else over. She did not seem concerned.

---

I ate leftover rice standing at the counter that night. The apartment was quiet in the specific way that a single person's apartment is quiet when bad news has moved in. Not the silence of peace. The silence of absence. No one to tell. No one to hold the other end of the weight.

I had a beer. Then another. Sat on the couch with Chipi on my chest and stared at the ceiling and counted days like a man counting bullets, trying to figure out how many I could afford to waste.

I thought about calling someone. Paco, maybe. But Paco would want to fix it and I wasn't ready for fixing yet. I was still in the part where you sit with the wreckage and catalogue what's broken before you start cleaning up.

I went to bed at midnight. The bed was too big. It's always been too big. I bought a queen when I moved in because it was on sale and every night since it's been a reminder that I sleep alone in a country that wasn't built for me, in a bed that was built for two.

---

The first week I was a machine.

Fourteen applications. I rebuilt my resume three times. Tailored cover letters for each company. Hit every job board, every LinkedIn connection, every Slack community for developers I'd ever lurked in. My portfolio was solid. Four years of clean backend work, good references, a system I'd architected at Brevity that handled 200,000 daily transactions without breaking a sweat.

Three companies responded. All three started warm. Phone screens that went well, technical conversations where I could hear the interviewer nodding along, that little pause of approval when I walked through my system design approach. Then the question.

"And your current visa status?"

The temperature change was instant. Every time. Like opening a freezer door. One recruiter literally said "Oh." Just that. A single syllable carrying the weight of an entire bureaucracy. She said they'd love to but they didn't have the infrastructure for H-1B transfers right now. The other two used almost identical language, which made me wonder if there was a template somewhere, a shared Google Doc titled "How to Reject Qualified Candidates Who Had the Audacity to Be Born in the Wrong Country."

Week two I adjusted my approach. Cast a wider net. Applied to seven more companies, including two I was overqualified for and one that was technically a downgrade in every way except the one that mattered, which was "willing to sponsor." Two interviews materialized. The first one, a fintech startup in the payments space, went beautifully. Three rounds. I crushed the technical assessment. The hiring manager and I talked for forty minutes about event-driven architecture and he was practically finishing my sentences. I walked out of that building feeling something I hadn't felt in two weeks. Hope. Stupid, dangerous, oxygen-tank-in-a-fire kind of hope.

He Googled the sponsorship costs that night. I know because his LinkedIn profile visited mine at 11:47 PM and his email the next morning started with "After careful consideration" which is corporate for "I looked up how much you'd cost me in legal fees and nearly choked on my dinner."

The second interview was with a consulting firm that said "we'll circle back in Q3." It was late Q1. Q3 was six months away. My visa didn't have six months.

I stopped telling anyone about the rejections after that. Who would I tell? My mother would panic. Héctor would say "come home." Paco would try to help and there was nothing to help with. The system wasn't broken. It was working exactly as designed. It just wasn't designed for me.

I started eating less. Not deliberately. The groceries just lasted longer when you don't feel like cooking and the bodega empanadas are three bucks and Lulu throws in an extra one when you look sad enough. I lost eight pounds in three weeks. My jeans got loose. Chipi got more of my dinner than I did some nights.

My mother called every Wednesday. Lupita González, fifty-eight years old, rosary in one hand and gossip in the other. She could sense something was off in my voice but I kept telling her everything was fine, work was busy. She always ended the call the same way. "¿Y cuándo me presentas a una novia?" When are you going to introduce me to a girlfriend? I'd laugh and say "pronto, mamá" and hang up and sit there feeling like the worst son in the history of Guadalajara.

My brother Héctor called too, but less often and with fewer words. Héctor is older by four years, career military, the kind of man who thinks feelings are a civilian luxury. He'd call and say "¿Todo bien?" and I'd say "Sí" and he'd say "Bueno" and that was it. Fifteen seconds of brotherhood in its most compressed form. I loved those calls. I also dreaded them because every "todo bien" was a lie and lying to Héctor felt like a specific kind of sin, the kind where you betray someone who would literally take a bullet for you by not trusting them with the truth.

Week three. My immigration lawyer Diana called on a Wednesday afternoon. I was on the couch in sweatpants I'd been wearing for two days with Chipi on my chest watching a cooking show I wasn't watching.

Diana is Colombian-American, mid-thirties, sharp in the way that good lawyers are sharp, which means she cuts clean and doesn't pretend it doesn't bleed. She'd been handling my green card application for over a year. She knew my case inside and out. She knew how much money I'd spent. She knew what was at stake.

"Manuel, I'm going to be straight with you."

"You always are."

"You have thirty-seven days. I can file for an extension based on extraordinary circumstances but the approval rate on those is about twelve percent and the processing time would eat your runway anyway. I cannot manufacture a sponsor for you. You need to find employment with a qualifying company or we need to start discussing voluntary departure."

Voluntary departure. Two words that sound administrative and mean: go home. Give up. Lose.

"Diana."

"I know."

"My green card application. Two years of paperwork. Eight thousand dollars."

"I know, Manuel."

I could hear the cooking show still playing in the background. Someone was making a soufflé. It seemed obscene that soufflés existed while my life was collapsing.

"Okay," I said. "Thirty-seven days."

"Call me the moment something changes. Any direction. I mean it."

I hung up. Chipi meowed. I picked her up and held her against my chest and she purred and for a full minute that was enough. Then it wasn't.

Thirty-seven days.

---

I'm not going to catalog every rejection after that. It would sound repetitive and honestly living it was repetitive, which is the worst part. Rejection doesn't get easier with practice. It gets duller. The sharp spike of the first few flattens into a constant low-grade hum of unworthiness that follows you around like tinnitus. You wake up with it. You brush your teeth with it. You sit at your laptop and write another cover letter with it buzzing behind your eyes.

I applied to forty-one companies in total. I got twelve first-round responses. Eight phone screens. Four technical interviews. Two second rounds. Zero offers.

The loneliness was the worst part. Not the rejections. Those had a structure: apply, wait, get rejected, apply again. The loneliness had no structure. It just sat in the apartment with me like a roommate who never talked and never left. I'd go days without a real conversation. Paco and I would text but texts aren't the same as someone sitting across from you while you eat. Lulu at the bodega was sometimes the only person who said my name out loud on a given day.

I started going to Danny's ramen shop at night just to sit at the counter and be around people. Danny never asked why I was there three nights a week. He just put a bowl in front of me and played K-pop too loud and let me exist.

I thought about dating apps. About swiping. About the specific cruelty of trying to be charming for a stranger when you're thirty days from deportation. I opened Hinge once, looked at it for four minutes, and closed it. What would my bio say? "Software developer (unemployed), Mexican (on a clock), looking for someone to hold me while my life falls apart." I deleted the app.

---

Day thirty-two on the clock. A Thursday. I had been twenty-nine days without a paycheck, without a sponsor, without a single concrete reason to believe things were going to work out.

I had three interviews that day. Three. I'd never managed to stack that many at once before. It felt like the universe was either giving me one last chance or setting me up for a triple knockout, and I wasn't sure which possibility frightened me more.

The first was at nine in the morning. A data infrastructure company downtown. The interviewer was a woman named Rachel who had kind eyes and a firm handshake and who told me within twelve minutes that they'd just filled the position internally but wanted to meet me anyway "for future opportunities." Future opportunities. The most American phrase in the English language. It means nothing.

The second interview was at eleven. A logistics tech company in a suburban office park. The technical lead was a guy named Scott who wore a fleece vest and spoke exclusively in acronyms. Then Scott's manager joined and said, "So, visa sponsorship. Walk me through the timeline and costs." I did. The manager nodded and said "let me take this back to leadership" and I knew.

The third interview was the one that mattered.

DataForge. A cloud security company I'd been in process with for three weeks. First round phone screen, aced it. Technical assessment, top marks. Second round with the engineering director, went great, real chemistry. She'd asked me about distributed systems and I'd drawn diagrams on the whiteboard and she'd nodded and said "this is exactly what we need."

They knew about the visa from the start. They said it wasn't a problem. The engineering director, Valerie, had specifically said, "We don't lose good engineers over paperwork."

Today was the final round. A "culture fit" conversation with their biggest enterprise client. I drove across town. Changed into my good shirt in the car. Called nobody because there was nobody to call.

The conference room had floor-to-ceiling windows and a long table and six people already seated. Valerie was there. She smiled at me. The client team lead was a man named Howard Kessler. Late fifties, silver hair, the kind of tan you get from golf, not sunshine.

The conversation started well. I walked them through my proposed architecture. Howard nodded along. Then he leaned back in his chair.

"Manuel, where are you from originally?"

"Guadalajara, Mexico."

"Beautiful city. My wife and I went to Puerto Vallarta last year." He paused. "And your work authorization situation. You're on an H-1B?"

Valerie jumped in. "Howard, we've discussed the sponsorship and our legal team is fully equipped to..."

"I'm sure they are." Howard kept looking at me. "I'm just asking for our own due diligence. Banking infrastructure. Client data. We need stability in the team. Long-term commitment."

He said "risk profile" like he was talking about a server, not a person.

The meeting lasted another twenty minutes. I performed. I answered every question. I smiled. Howard told me it was great to meet me. Valerie walked me to the elevator and her face told me everything her mouth didn't say yet.

"I'm sorry, Manuel. I'm going to fight for this."

"I know."

She called the next morning. "Howard's team flagged the visa as an unacceptable risk variable. I pushed back. Hard. He sided with the client."

I hung up. Put the phone face-down on the table. Stared at my cereal. Chipi walked across the table and sat on my phone like she knew I didn't want to look at it.

Day twenty-eight.

---

I needed to get out of the apartment.

Not from anyone. From the walls. From the sweatpants and the cereal bowl and the silence that had become a physical presence. I grabbed my bag and headed to CraftWork, the coworking space I'd been using since the layoff. Exposed ductwork, overpriced drip coffee, a vending machine full of sparkling water that cost four dollars a can.

I found a spot in the back corner. Opened my laptop. Stared at a job board. The listings blurred.

"Hermano. You look like you're trying to dig a hole with a spoon."

Paco Herrera. Twenty-eight. Venezuelan. Fellow developer, fellow immigrant, fellow member of the "people whose lives depend on paperwork filed by strangers" club. I'd met him here three weeks ago. He had one of those faces that's always on the edge of a joke, dark eyes that stayed amused even when he was serious, and a voice that carried whether he wanted it to or not. He was holding two coffees.

"You look like you need this more than I do." He set one in front of me and dropped into the chair across the table without being invited. That was Paco. He treated social boundaries like loose suggestions.

"Thanks."

"How many today?"

"How many what?"

"Rejections."

I looked at him. He raised his eyebrows. "Brother, I can read it on your face. You've got that look. The 'I just got told I'm too expensive to exist in this country' look."

"Three interviews yesterday. Three nos."

"Mierda." He leaned back. "All visa?"

"The last one wasn't even about the visa directly. The client just looked at me and saw a risk profile."

"A risk profile." Paco said it the way you'd say a slur. "You know what's a risk profile? Hiring some mediocre gringo who'll leave for a ten percent raise in six months."

He wasn't wrong. I drank the coffee.

"How many days?" he asked.

"Twenty-eight."

Paco whistled low. He knew the math.

"Listen," he said, and his voice shifted. "I'm going to tell you about something and I need you to not dismiss it immediately."

"Depends on what it is."

"There's a company. Shape of You Inc. My cousin's friend worked there for six months. Made more money than he'd made in two years writing code."

"Doing what?"

"That's where it gets interesting." Paco leaned forward. "You ever heard of consciousness transfer?"

I stared at him.

"Don't look at me like that. It's real. It's regulated. The company takes your consciousness and puts it in another body. A clone body. You do a job in that body, you come back, you get paid."

"That sounds insane."

"It is insane. It's also real and here's the part that matters to you." He pointed his coffee cup at me. "They have their own legal entity. They can issue employment verification. They've sponsored people before under some specialized visa classification. Biometric research consultant or something."

"Paco."

"I know how it sounds."

"It sounds like a scam."

"My cousin's friend bought a car with what he made there. A Mazda 3 with heated seats."

He texted me the address. Shape of You Inc.

"I have twenty-eight days," I said.

"Twenty-eight days and a spoon," Paco said. "I'm offering you a shovel."

---

I went home. Sat on the bed. Googled "Shape of You Inc." on my phone.

The website was minimal. Stark white background. A logo that made my eyes do something uncomfortable: a face inside a face inside a face, nested, recursive. No mission statement. No team page. Just a single line: "Walk-in consultations available. 814 Ninth Street. Hours: 9 AM to 5 PM weekdays."

I tried calling. Automated system. "Our offices are currently closed. Walk-in consultations are available during business hours."

I looked at the time. 4:17 PM. Too late to go today.

Tomorrow.

I set the phone down. Lay back on the bed. Chipi jumped up and curled against my hip. The apartment was quiet. No one waiting for me to explain. No one to convince. No one to consult.

That's the thing about being single in a crisis. Every decision is yours alone. The freedom of that is also the weight of it. There's no one to talk you into courage and no one to talk you out of fear and the space between those two things is the exact width of an empty bed.

Twenty-eight days. A career in ashes. A family in Guadalajara waiting for good news I can't deliver and a door on Ninth Street that might lead somewhere or nowhere.

I didn't have the luxury of nowhere.

Tomorrow I go to Ninth Street.
