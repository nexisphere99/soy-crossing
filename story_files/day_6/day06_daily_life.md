Ninth Street to Third Ave, past the laundromat that's never open when you need it, past the Dominican hair salon with the music always spilling out, past the bodega that isn't Lulu's and therefore doesn't count. My neighborhood. Six blocks of New York that feel like mine in a way the rest of the city doesn't.

Twenty-two days on the visa clock. But Diana said "very strong chance." Patricia Kwan at SoY has done this before. Four H-1Bs transferred successfully. If I pass the assessment on Thursday, the petition gets filed. The receipt notice comes in two to three weeks. The clock stops.

For the first time in a month, the countdown feels less like a fuse and more like a timer. The same mechanism, pointed toward a different outcome.

I turn left on Fifth. Danny's neon sign blinks in the window, half the letters working, the R in RAMEN permanently dark. The K-pop is audible from the sidewalk. Tonight it's something loud and percussive that shakes the glass.

Tommy's already at the counter. I can see his profile through the window, shoulders hunched over a menu he already knows by heart, one foot bouncing on the stool rung.

I push through the door.

The ramen steam hits first. Then the music. Then Danny, behind the counter, grinning.

"Manuel! Saturday special. Extra chashu for people who look like they've had a week."

"I've had a week."

"Then you get two extra."

Tommy turns. Nods. The nod of a man who's been through something with you that neither of you can explain to anyone else in this restaurant.

I sit down. The counter stool is warm from the kitchen heat. The menu is laminated and sticky at the corners. The broth is already simmering. My week is over. Saturday is here.

And for the next three hours, I don't have to think about bodies that aren't mine.
