## SCENE 1: TOMMY AT DANNY'S (SQ-007 Resolution)

### PASSAGE: t0_day06_dannys_tommy

**Time: 7:00 PM | Location: Danny's Ramen**

Tommy is three bites into the habanero ramen when I sit down. His eyes are watering. His nose is running. He looks like a man who's lost a fight with a pepper and refuses to concede.

"How is it?"

He holds up a finger. Chews. Swallows. Breathes through his mouth like a woman in labor. "It's good."

"You're crying."

"These are flavor tears. Danny, can I get water?"

Danny appears with water and a grin that suggests he increased the heat level without being asked. He slides a menu at me. "Spicy miso for Manuel?"

"Please."

"Extra chashu?"

"Always."

Danny disappears. The K-pop tonight is BTS, something upbeat and relentless, the bass vibrating through the counter. Two other customers sit at the far end, a couple sharing a bowl, their chopsticks occasionally colliding.

Tommy pushes the water glass aside, reaches for the ramen again. He's committed now.

"So." He sniffs. "Saturday. First one off."

"How are you spending it?"

"Woke up at six because my body forgot weekends exist. Went for a run. Showered. Sat on the couch for two hours staring at the wall." He takes another bite. More tears. "Really productive stuff."

"I cleaned my apartment."

"Big day for both of us."

We sit with it for a second. The music fills the gap. Danny puts my miso down, steam rising off the surface in a way that looks like the broth is thinking.

"Can I ask you something?" Tommy says.

"Sure."

"The speculum." He puts his chopsticks down. "How long did it take you to stop thinking about it?"

"I'll let you know when it happens."

He nods. Not relieved exactly. Validated. "I keep, it's not the pain. The pain was, whatever, it was uncomfortable. It's the, the openness. Being opened. Having someone look inside you." He picks at a noodle. "My girlfriend got a pap smear last month. She mentioned it like she was mentioning a haircut. 'Got a pap smear, stopped at Target, bought shampoo.' Like it was nothing."

"Because for her it is nothing. It's Tuesday."

"It's every Tuesday." He shakes his head. "I watched my girlfriend shrug off something that kept me awake last night, and she doesn't even know I did it, and she'll never know, and I can't tell her."

Danny refills Tommy's water without being asked. Good instincts.

"Georgia said something," I tell him. "Day 5. She said understanding someone better isn't the same as being understood back."

Tommy lets that sit. He eats. I eat. The ramen is perfect, rich, salty, the chashu soft enough to dissolve, the egg yolk liquid gold.

"Can I tell you something else?" he says.

"Yeah."

"When I was in Renata. When I stood up and, and felt the weight. The hips, the chest, the way the body just, occupies space differently." He takes a breath. "I liked it. Not sexually. Not like that. I liked the, the authority of it. The way that body owned a room just by standing in it."

"Renata's body is built like confidence took a physical form."

"Exactly. So I'm lying in bed last night thinking about that. About liking it. About what it means that I liked being in a woman's body that felt powerful. Does it mean I want to be a woman? No. I don't think so. Does it mean I'm, I don't know, something?"

"Something."

"Yeah. Something." He finishes the habanero ramen. Every drop. Sets the bowl down with the quiet triumph of a man who has survived.

"Tommy."

"Yeah?"

"I think we're all 'something' after this week. I don't think the word for it exists yet."

He laughs. First real laugh I've heard from him. It's short, surprised, like a dog that didn't know it could bark.

"Manuel?"

"Yeah?"

"I'm glad you're in the cohort. Georgia's great but she's, she already gets it, you know? She's ahead of us. You and me, we're figuring it out at the same speed. That helps."

"Me too."

Danny comes by. Dessert: mochi ice cream, green tea flavor. On the house because "Saturday customers are my favorites."

We eat mochi. The music switches to something slower, a ballad. Tommy tells me about his daughter. Three years old. Calls everything "big." Big dog. Big car. Big moon. He shows me a photo on his phone and her face is round and grinning and she's holding a stuffed elephant that's larger than she is.

"She's why I'm doing this," he says. "The money. Day care costs more than my rent."

"My visa is why I'm doing this. But I'd be lying if I said the work hasn't changed me."

"Yeah." He pockets the phone. "Yeah, it has."

We split the check. Tommy reaches for his wallet. I let him, because he said "I'm buying" in the text, and sometimes letting someone pay is a way of accepting what they're offering, which is more than dinner.

Outside. The cold hits. We stand on the sidewalk under Danny's broken neon sign, the R in RAMEN still dark, our breath making clouds.

"Monday," he says.

"Monday."

"Elena's going to destroy us."

"That's her job."

He nods. Puts his hands in his pockets. "Hey. Thanks for this. I needed, you know."

"I know."

He walks to his bus stop. I watch him go, shoulders up against the cold, a man with a three-year-old who calls everything big and a week of female bodies behind him and a week more ahead.

I head toward Vega.

## SCENE 2: PACO AT VEGA (SQ-010 Advancement)

### PASSAGE: t0_day06_vega_paco

**Time: 9:15 PM | Location: Vega Bar**

Vega on a Saturday is a different animal than Vega on a weeknight. The music is louder, Latin house instead of the usual soft salsa. The crowd is thicker. Bodies at the bar, bodies at the tables, bodies moving between. The lighting is dim enough to hide flaws and bright enough to find beauty, which is a bartender's trick that Adriana probably engineered herself.

Paco is in our usual corner. He's got a rum and Coke already half-done and the look on his face that means he's either in love or he's had an idea, which for Paco are usually the same thing.

"Hermano!" He stands, hugs me, the kind of back-slapping embrace that Venezuelans deploy like punctuation. "Sit, sit. I ordered you one already."

Rum and Coke appears. The rum is good. Paco always brings the good rum, Diplomático, the bottle half-empty because he started at home.

"How's the week?" he asks, even though he knows how the week is because I've been texting him about it in fragments.

"Long. Good. Intense."

"The body stuff."

"The body stuff."

He leans in. "So, I have a question. When you're in the woman body." He gestures vaguely at his own chest. "The boobs. What's that like?"

"Paco."

"I'm asking scientifically."

"You are not asking scientifically."

"I'm asking with scientific curiosity and personal interest. Come on. You can't do what you're doing and not tell your best friend about the boobs."

I drink. Sigh. "It's weight. Constant weight. Like wearing a backpack on your front. The body adjusts, the spine curves, the shoulders roll. You don't notice it until you take them off. I mean, until you de-transfer. Then your chest is flat and it feels like something's missing."

"Oh no."

"Valentina."

"Paco."

"She lives in Brooklyn. Bay Ridge. She's Isabela's friend from university. She's a graphic designer, she speaks three languages, she likes cooking, and she asked about you."

"How can she ask about me? She's never met me."

"Isabela told her about you. I may have provided supplementary information."

"That you're tall, Mexican, smart, employed," he counts on his fingers, "and recently started a mysterious job that involves women's bodies, which I presented as 'biotech research' because I'm not a complete idiot."

## SCENE 3: ADRIANA AT VEGA (SQ-009 Major Advancement)

### PASSAGE: t0_day06_vega_adriana

**Time: 11:20 PM | Location: Vega Bar**

Paco leaves at 11. He's texting Isabela before he's out the door, the phone's light illuminating his face as he walks into the cold, grinning at something she sent.

I should leave too. It's late. Chipi is home alone, probably orchestrating a complex heist involving the butter dish. But the bar is warm and the music has shifted to something slower, a cumbia that moves through the room like smoke, and the mezcal is sitting in my stomach like a small, comfortable fire.

Adriana is working the bar. Saturday night means she's busy, pulling taps, shaking cocktails, moving between customers with the efficiency of someone who's done this long enough that her body works on a different operating system than her brain. She talks to a couple at the far end. She laughs with a regular. She wipes the bar in long, practiced strokes.

She sees me looking. Not watching. Looking. There's a difference. Watching is passive. Looking is a choice.

She comes over when the rush thins. Pours mezcal without asking. The espadín. Smoky, a little sweet. Good for people who are carrying heavy things and pretending they're not.

"Saturday regular." She puts the glass down. "Paco left?"

"Paco left."

"He's loud. I like him. He tips well and he makes the bar feel full even when it's empty."

"That's Paco. Takes up more space than his body should allow."

She leans on the bar. The tattoo sleeves catch the dim light, geometric patterns on her right arm, flowers and vines on her left. Up close I can see the detail, a hummingbird hiding in the flowers on the left forearm, so small you'd miss it if you weren't looking.

"Is that a hummingbird?"

She looks down. Turns her arm. "You're the first person who's noticed without me pointing it out."

"It's hiding."

"That's the point. My abuela used to say, the hummingbird is the only bird that can fly backward. She said it meant you can always go back to where you started." She traces the bird with one finger. "I got it when I dropped out of nursing school. Reminder that going backward isn't the same as failing."

"Why'd you drop out?"

"Because I was good at the science and terrible at the distance. You're supposed to, you know, maintain professional detachment. Patient in room four has stage four pancreatic cancer, and you chart it, and you check the drip, and you move to room five. I couldn't do room five." She picks up a glass, polishes it. The motion is automatic, hands doing one thing while her mind does another. "I kept staying in room four."

"So you became a bartender."

"Bartending is nursing for people who aren't dying. Same skills. Listen, diagnose, prescribe." She holds up a bottle. "The prescription is just more fun."

I drink. The mezcal is warm. She's warm. The bar is warm. The music is warm. Everything is warm and I'm sitting here with a week of bodies behind me and a bartender in front of me who notices hummingbirds and stayed in room four and I want to tell her everything.

I don't.

"Can I ask you something?" I say instead.

"That's my line."

"When you read people. At the bar. What do you see when you look at me?"

She studies me. Not quickly. She takes her time. Dark eyes moving across my face the way a doctor reads a chart, or the way a bartender reads a customer, which might be the same skill in different lighting.

"I see someone who's carrying something heavy that he chose to carry. Not dumped on him. Chosen. That's different. People who got dumped on look angry. People who chose it look, careful."

"Careful."

"Like every sentence is load-bearing. Like if you say the wrong thing, something collapses." She tilts her head. "But you also look like someone who just figured out the load is getting lighter. The last few times you've been in here, your shoulders have been lower. You're breathing deeper. Whatever you're carrying, you're getting better at carrying it."

My chest tightens. Not the phantom. Something else. The specific tightness of being seen by someone who isn't trying to fix you, just read you.

"You're good at this."

"I've had practice." She pours herself a measure of the espadín. A small one, a working-shift allowance. "To Saturday regulars."

"To bartenders who stayed in room four."

We drink. The eye contact lasts three seconds. Four. Enough.
