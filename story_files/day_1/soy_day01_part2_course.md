## PASSAGE: t0_day01_facility_arrival

The door at 814 Ninth Street is heavier than it looks. Brushed steel, no handle. I push and it gives, smooth and silent, and the air changes instantly. Outside: October cold, diesel exhaust, the hum of the city at 8:22 AM. Inside: temperature-controlled cool, the faint hum of machinery behind walls, a smell like ozone and new carpet and something underneath both that I can't name. Something sterile. Something alive.

The lobby is small. White walls, white floor, recessed lighting that makes everything look like a photograph someone adjusted for clarity. A single desk. Behind it, a woman who radiates efficiency the way other people radiate warmth.

"Name."

"Manuel González."

She doesn't look up from her screen. She's already typing. Short, compact, black hair in a high ponytail with a streak of silver that looks intentional. Enamel pins on her jacket lapel; today's selection includes a tiny cat and what might be a DNA helix. Augmented lenses with a faint blue tint. She types like she's angry at the keyboard and the keyboard deserves it.

"ID."

I hand over my driver's license. She scans it, swipes it through something built into the desk, and hands it back in one fluid motion. Her eyes flick to mine for the first time. Dark, quick, assessing.

"Yuki Tanaka. I handle intake, scheduling, and making sure nobody dies or misses lunch, which are roughly equal priorities around here. You'll get a badge and a biometric registration today. Badge opens doors. Biometric opens everything else." She slides a tablet across the desk. "Intake forms. Fill these out. All of them. Don't skip the allergen section. People always skip the allergen section and then they transfer into a body with a shellfish allergy and eat a shrimp and we have a situation."

"Has that happened?"

"Twice." She says it the way you'd say "Tuesday."

I fill out the forms. Medical history, emergency contact (Paco, because who else), consent acknowledgments that scroll for pages. Some of them are standard. Some of them include phrases I've never seen on any form in my life: "neuroelectric mapping consent," "consciousness compartmentalization waiver," "temporary somatic reassignment liability release."

I sign everything. My hand is steady. The rest of me isn't.

Yuki takes the tablet back, processes it, and hands me a badge. White plastic, my photo pulled from the scan (I look startled; fair), my name in small letters, and a green stripe across the top.

"Green means candidate. You'll see red stripes later, that's assessment phase. If you make it that far." She pauses, and something almost human crosses her face. "Room 4. Down the hall, second left. Don't be late. She hates late."

"She?"

But Yuki is already back to her screen, fingers hammering keys. I've been processed. I take my badge, clip it to my henley, and walk down the hall.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_elena_entrance

The room rearranges itself.

That's the only way I can describe it. Elena Cordero walks through the door and the air pressure changes, or the lighting shifts, or something molecular happens that makes every person in every chair sit up straighter without deciding to.

She's tall. Lean. Angular in a way that's less fashion and more architecture. Everything about her is load-bearing, nothing decorative. Black hair cut short at the jaw, tucked behind one ear. Brown skin, sharp cheekbones, dark eyes that sweep the room the way a scalpel sweeps tissue: with purpose and without hesitation. She wears a fitted charcoal blazer over a black turtleneck. No jewelry except a single silver ring on her left thumb. She moves like her hands still remember surgery, precise, economical, every gesture deliberate.

She stops at the center of the semicircle. Doesn't introduce herself yet. Just stands there and looks at each of us, one by one, for long enough that it's uncomfortable. Her eyes land on me. Stay there for one second, two, three. Then move on.

Brett shifts in his seat. The energy drink crinkles.

"My name is Elena Cordero. I am the primary instructor for this cohort. For the next six working days, possibly eight if remediation is required, I am responsible for teaching you the science, ethics, law, and practice of consciousness transfer. I am also responsible for determining whether you should be allowed anywhere near another person's body."

She pauses. The pause isn't for emphasis. It's a test. She's watching who flinches.

"Sixty percent of candidates who sit in these chairs do not complete the program. That is not a boast. That is data. You are not here because you are special. You are here because you applied and were not immediately disqualified. Whether you leave here employed or dismissed is entirely dependent on what happens in these rooms over the next week."

She touches the screen behind her. It flickers to life.

"Let us begin."

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_lecture_begin

The screen shows a diagram. A human silhouette, gender-neutral, featureless, with a glowing web of lines extending from the brain through the entire body. The lines pulse in slow rhythms. Neural pathways rendered in blue-white light.

"What you are looking at," Elena says, "is a complete neuroelectric map of a human consciousness. Not the brain alone. The entire cognitive-emotional-somatic identity. Every memory you hold, every reflex you have trained, every preference, phobia, habit, and instinct. All of it is encoded in this network. It extends from the cerebral cortex through the spinal column, through the peripheral nervous system, into your fingertips and your toes and the pit of your stomach where you feel dread."

She lets that land. I write it down on my phone. My fingers are cold.

"Consciousness transfer is the process of compressing this entire network into a transferable signal and installing it into a compatible neural substrate. That substrate can be a clone body, a lab-grown vessel with a functioning nervous system but no consciousness, no identity, no person inside. Or it can be a host body, a real human being who has consented, contractually and biometrically, to having your consciousness inhabit their form for a specified duration and purpose."

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_lecture_methods

The screen changes. Three pods, shown in cross-section. Each one different.

"There are three methods of transfer. You will use one of them tomorrow. You should understand all three."

She touches the first image. A large chamber filled with blue-green liquid, a human body floating inside, cables attached at the temples, spine, and wrists.

"Type I. The immersion chamber. Full-body pod, liquid suspension. This is the oldest method, developed twelve years ago, still the most stable. You are physically submerged. The liquid is a conductive medium that allows simultaneous neural mapping at every point on the body. Calibration time is longest, forty to sixty minutes, but the transfer itself is the smoothest. You barely feel the transition. One moment you're in the liquid, the next you're in a different body. It's used for first-time transfers at some facilities, though not this one, and for high-risk assignments where stability is paramount."

She touches the second image. A chair with a headset apparatus that cups the skull and extends prongs to the temples and the base of the neck.

"Type II. The neural headset. Seated. Faster. Calibration in twelve to twenty minutes. This is the industry standard for working transferees. You'll sit in a chair, the headset maps your neural architecture through focused contact points, compresses your consciousness, and installs it in the target body. The transition feels like..." She pauses. Looks at us. "I could describe it. But description is insufficient preparation. You'll feel it tomorrow. For now, understand that it involves thermal shift, pressure sensation, and a brief period of perceptual compression. Candidates describe it as being pulled through something impossibly narrow, followed by sudden expansion."

Tommy's knee is bouncing. I can see it from the corner of my eye.

She touches the third image. Something like a coffin, sleek and dark, with monitoring equipment along the sides.

"Type III. The sleep capsule. Unconscious transfer. You fall asleep in your body and wake up in another. Used for long-duration swaps and for transferees who have medical contraindications to conscious transfer. It carries a higher disorientation risk on waking. You have no transitional awareness, no compression experience; you simply open your eyes somewhere else. Some candidates find this easier. Some find it profoundly disturbing. We don't use it in Tier 0."

She kills the screen. Turns back to us.

"Questions."

Not a question. A command.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_lecture_questions

Brett raises his energy drink like a hand. "What happens if the transfer fails? Like, mid-transfer?"

Elena looks at him. Her expression doesn't change but something in the room gets colder.

"Define 'fails.'"

"I don't know, like, the signal gets interrupted. Do I just... stop existing?"

"Transfer failure rates at this facility are point-zero-three percent. In the event of a disruption, the standard failsafe returns your consciousness to your original body within eleven seconds. You will experience disorientation, nausea, and potentially a migraine that lasts up to forty-eight hours. You will not 'stop existing.' The process has redundancies built on redundancies. Your consciousness is copied, not moved. Your body retains a dormant image of your neural map during the entire transfer duration. If the active signal fails, the dormant copy wakes."

"So my body just... lies there? While I'm in the other one?"

"Your body enters a maintenance state. Autonomous functions continue: heartbeat, respiration, digestion. But your consciousness is not present. Your body is, for that duration, an empty vessel. Same as the clones."

She says this flatly. Like it's information and not the most existentially horrifying sentence I've heard in my life. I type it into my phone. My hands are definitely not steady anymore.

Georgia raises her hand. Actually raises it. Calm, composed.

"You said most high-paying assignments involve male-to-female transfer. What's the ratio?"

Elena nods. "Approximately seventy percent of Tier 1 and above assignments are male consciousness into female bodies. The reasons are economic and social rather than scientific. Female bodies are in higher demand for surrogate assignments: medical appointments, social obligations, physical experiences that the host wishes to delegate. The market pays accordingly."

I write that down. Seventy percent. The number sits on my phone screen like a fact about someone else's life that has accidentally wandered into mine.

"Will we be transferring into female bodies?" Tommy asks. His voice is tight. Controlled. A man trying hard to sound casual about something that is not casual at all.

"All of you will experience cross-gender transfer during this training. It is not optional. The market requires it and your employment depends on competency in it."

Tommy nods. Keeps nodding. Nods about three times more than necessary. I recognize the gesture. I've done it myself. The nod that says "I understand" while the brain behind it screams.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_lecture_science_2

Elena brings the screen back. New diagram, two bodies side by side, connected by flowing lines of light, with data readouts along the margins.

"Compatibility. Not every consciousness fits every body. Neural architecture matching accounts for the fundamental structure. Your brain's wiring patterns must be compatible with the target body's nervous system. Think of it as a plug fitting a socket. Most human neural architectures are compatible with most human bodies, but edge cases exist. You will be tested."

She scrolls. Body mass differential charts.

"Body mass differential tolerance. Your consciousness was shaped by your body. You have spatial awareness, proprioception, a sense of your own size that is calibrated to your current form. Transferring into a body that is significantly larger or smaller than your own creates a recalibration period. For most male-to-female transfers, the differential is manageable. You'll lose height, weight, and mass, and gain a lower center of gravity. It takes thirty to ninety seconds for your consciousness to adjust. This is called the calibration window."

She looks directly at me. I don't know why. I keep typing.

"Hormonal environment adjustment. This is the one that surprises people." Her voice gets quieter. Not softer; quieter. Like she's delivering information she wants to make sure lands. "Female bodies run on different hormonal profiles. Estrogen, progesterone, varying levels of testosterone. When your consciousness enters a female body, you inherit the hormonal environment of that body. This affects emotional responsiveness, pain sensitivity, temperature perception, and arousal thresholds. You will feel things differently. Not more. Not less. Differently. And you will need to learn the difference between what the body is feeling and what you are choosing to feel."

The room is very still.

"The seventy-two-hour window." She puts a chart on screen, a curve that rises gradually and then drops off a cliff. "Maximum continuous transfer duration. Beyond seventy-two hours in a non-native body, the risk of psychological degradation escalates sharply. Identity blurring, emotional memory contamination, phantom sensation persistence, and in extreme cases, a condition we call drift: a progressive inability to distinguish your identity from the body's baseline state. Every assignment you take in your career will be time-locked well below this threshold. Any questions about why should be directed at the case studies you'll review in Module 4."

She shuts the screen off again. Crosses her arms.

"We have covered transfer methods, compatibility, and duration limits. Now we address the two categories of body you will inhabit."

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_lecture_clones

"Clones." Elena says the word without inflection. No drama, no weight. A category.

"Lab-grown bodies with functional nervous systems. They have hearts that beat, lungs that breathe, skin that sweats, stomachs that digest. They have reflexes, motor responses, sensory input pathways. What they do not have is consciousness. No identity. No memories. No person. A clone body is a vessel. You will train in clones during this program, three of them, each with a different physical profile and a different neural complexity. They are tools. You will treat them with mechanical respect, the way a pilot respects an aircraft. Not because the aircraft has feelings, but because carelessness with the machine gets people killed."

She walks to the side of the room. There's a small display case I hadn't noticed before, three photographs, each showing a woman from the shoulders up. They look real. They look alive. They look like people I might pass on the street.

"These are your training clones. You'll meet them in sequence. For now, know that they exist, and that inhabiting them will be the most disorienting experience of your life to date."

She returns to center.

"Hosts." Her voice shifts. Not warmer, but more careful. Like she's handling something fragile and sharp. "Hosts are real people. Living, conscious human beings who consent to having a transferee occupy their body for a contracted purpose. Hosts retain full legal rights over their body at all times. The contract governs what you may do, where you may go, what you may touch, who you may speak to. Violating the terms of a host contract is not a workplace infraction. It is, depending on severity, a criminal act."

She holds the silence for three full seconds.

"The distinction between a clone and a host is the most important concept you will learn in this room. A clone is an instrument. A host is a person. Confusing the two, treating a host like a thing or a clone like a person, is the mistake that ends careers and starts lawsuits. I will teach you to navigate both. But I cannot teach you the instinct to care. That you bring with you or you don't."

She checks her watch. The silver ring glints.

"Break. Fifteen minutes. Water and restrooms are down the hall to the left. Be back in this room at ten forty-five or don't come back at all."

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_break

The hallway outside Room 4 has a water cooler and a bench and the specific institutional calm of a place designed for humans to decompress between sessions that have compressed them. The water is cold. I drink two cups. My hands are still cold but the rest of me is overheating with information.

Tommy appears next to me at the water cooler. Up close, he's even bigger. Six-two, maybe two hundred and twenty pounds. He fills a cup and drinks it in one motion, crushing the paper in his fist like it owed him money.

"You good?" he says.

"Processing."

"Yeah." He fills another cup. "I heard 'seventy percent of jobs are male-to-female' and my brain just went..." He makes a sound like a computer error. "Bzzzt."

"Same."

"What's your deal? Why are you here?"

The question is too direct for a fifteen-minute break between strangers, but there's something about Tommy's delivery, blunt, unpolished, the cadence of a guy who never learned to small-talk because he skipped straight to big-talk, that makes it easy to answer.

"Visa. I need a sponsor or I leave the country in twenty-seven days."

His eyebrows go up. "Shit."

"Yeah."

"That's..." He stops himself. Drinks more water. "I'm Tommy. EMT. Or I was. I froze on a call. Kid in a car accident. Couldn't move. They put me on leave. Can't go back until I prove I can handle crisis situations without locking up."

He says it fast, like ripping a bandage, and looks at the floor when he's done.

"This is your crisis situation proof?"

"If I can handle having my brain put in a woman's body by strangers, I can handle a Tuesday night fender-bender. That's the theory."

I think about that. The math of it. Trading one kind of impossible for another.

"I'm Manuel."

"Tommy." We've already said our names but the repetition makes it a handshake.

Georgia passes us on her way back to Room 4. She doesn't stop. Doesn't need to. Her stride is the statement.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_rafael_intro

The afternoon belongs to Rafael Mende.

If Elena is a scalpel, Rafael is warm water. He walks into Room 4 after the break and the room temperature goes up two degrees, not literally, but the effect is equivalent. He's medium height, broad-shouldered, solid in a way that's going soft at the edges like a book that's been well-read. "Good afternoon. I'm Rafael Mende. I will be conducting your psychological evaluations throughout the program. I'm also the person who decides whether you advance, repeat, or go home." He smiles when he says this. The smile is genuine. That makes it worse. "I'll be meeting each of you individually this afternoon. Ninety minutes each. The rest of you are free to use the cafeteria, the reading room, or the break area until your slot. Don't leave the building."

He looks at his tablet. "Manuel. You're first."

Of course I am.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_rafael_session

Rafael's office is warmer than the rest of the facility. There are actual plants, a fern, a succulent, something with wide green leaves that might be a rubber tree. A bookshelf with titles I can half-read: clinical psychology texts, military memoirs, a collection of Brazilian poetry. The chair he offers me is leather, worn, comfortable in a way that's designed to make you drop your guard.

I sit. He sits across from me. Not behind a desk; two chairs facing each other, a small table between them with a glass of water and a tablet he barely looks at.

"So. Manuel."

"Yeah."

"First name basis here. Always. I'm Rafael. Not Dr. Mende, not sir. Rafael." He settles into his chair like a man who plans to be there a while. "Tell me why you're here."

"I need a visa."

"That's the circumstance. I'm asking about the choice. You could have spent today applying for more software jobs. Instead you're in a building where people transfer consciousness between bodies. Why this?"

"Because forty-one software companies said no and I'm running out of days."

He nods. Writes nothing down. "How many days?"

"Twenty-seven."

"And the H-1B, your employer was Brevity Labs?"

"Was. They laid off the entire backend team. Six people. My badge stopped working before I got to the parking lot."

He looks at me steadily. No pity. Something adjacent to pity that's more useful: attention. Real attention. The kind that doesn't flinch away from uncomfortable things.

"Tell me about your family."

"My parents are in Guadalajara. My father, Ricardo, retired mechanic. My mother, Lupita, she lights candles at church for me every week. My brother Héctor is military."

"Do they know about your situation?"

"My mother knows I lost the job. She doesn't know the rest. The visa clock. She'd..." I stop. "She'd panic. And then she'd pray. And neither of those helps."

"And your brother?"

"Héctor thinks I'm soft for choosing computers over something real. He's not wrong."

Rafael almost smiles. "I doubt that."

He shifts in his chair. Crosses his ankle over his knee. Casual. Deliberate.

"Relationship status?"

"Single."

"How long?"

"Since I moved here. Four years."

He writes something down. First thing he's written. I try to read it upside down. Can't.

"Dating?"

"Hard to date when your residence in the country is conditional."

"Tell me more about that."

I look at the rubber tree plant. Its leaves are shiny and thick and it looks absolutely certain of itself in a way I envy.

"You put a timeline on everything. Every conversation becomes 'does this person know I might not be here next year?' Every date becomes a countdown. At some point you stop trying because the math doesn't work. You can't ask someone to build something with you when the foundation might dissolve."

He nods. Doesn't rush. Lets the silence sit between us like a third person in the room.

"Any history of gender-related curiosity? And I ask that without judgment; it's safety data, not moral data."

"I don't think so. No."

"You said 'I don't think so.' Not 'no.'"

"I said no."

"Okay." He writes something else. "Have you ever wondered what it would feel like to be in a different body?"

"Hasn't everyone?"

"Not everyone. Some people are very comfortable in exactly the body they have. Some people have never considered the question. I'm asking whether you have."

I think about it. Actually think about it, not just produce an answer shaped like thinking.

"I've wondered what it would be like to not be in this specific body. In this specific situation. In this specific chair. But I don't think that's the same thing."

He tilts his head. "Maybe not. Maybe it is." He touches the compass tattoo on his wrist. "I'm going to ask you some standard assessment questions now. Some of them will feel invasive. Answer honestly, not because honesty is a virtue, but because the telemetry during your first transfer will tell me whether you lied, and neither of us wants that conversation."

The next hour is clinical. Dissociation tendency: he asks me to describe a moment I felt disconnected from my own body. I describe the moment HR walked me to my desk at Brevity. "It was like watching someone else pack a box with my things in it." He nods.

Stress response patterns: he asks about the worst moment of the last month. I describe the DataForge interview. Howard Kessler and his "risk profile." The tan, the golf-course confidence, the way "risk variable" sounded in his mouth.

"You got angry."

"I got quiet. I get quiet when I'm angry."

"And when you're scared?"

"I get loud."

"Interesting. Usually it's the reverse."

Empathy calibration: he shows me photographs. Faces expressing emotions at various intensities. I identify them. He marks my speed. I'm fast on sadness, slow on contempt. He notes this.

Sexual response awareness: this one makes my face hot. He asks whether I've ever been aroused in a context that surprised me. I say yes and he asks me to describe it and I describe a dream I had in college where I was someone else, gender unspecified, and woke up hard and confused. He writes it down without blinking.

Then he sets his tablet on the table. Leans forward. The casual posture drops and what's underneath is solid steel wrapped in corduroy.

"Manuel. I'm going to be direct. In approximately sixteen hours you're going to sit in a chair and someone is going to put your consciousness into the body of a woman. A clone, not a person, but the body will be female in every physiological sense. Breasts. Hips. Genitalia configured differently from yours. A hormonal environment you have never experienced. You will feel things you have never felt. The body will have responses you did not choose and cannot fully control. This is not hypothetical. This is tomorrow."

The water glass on the table trembles slightly. It takes me a second to realize I'm the one shaking the table. My knee. Bouncing.

"I know."

"Do you have any questions for me? About any of it?"

I look at the compass on his wrist. It points in every direction. That seems right.

"Will I still be me? After?"

He holds my gaze. The warmth in his face doesn't disappear. It deepens, like a fire settling into its coals.

"That's the best question anyone has asked me in this room in six months. And the honest answer is: yes, and also you will be more than you were. Whether that's a good thing depends on what you do with it."

He stands. Extends his hand. I shake it. His grip is firm and his palm is warm and the compass tattoo faces me as our hands clasp.

"Get some rest tonight, Manuel. Read chapters one through three of the handbook. And eat something. You look thin."

"Everyone keeps telling me to eat."

"Then eat." He smiles. "I'll see you tomorrow. We start early."

I walk out of his office. The hallway is quiet. Tommy is waiting on the bench, bouncing his knee the way I was bouncing mine thirty seconds ago. He looks up.

"How bad?"

"He's... thorough."

"Great." Tommy stands. Cracks his knuckles. "If I cry in there, it never happened."

"Agreed."

He walks in. The door closes.

I stand in the hallway for a minute. My phone says 4:47 PM. The Transfer Worker's Handbook has appeared in my inbox, a PDF, 340 pages, chapters titled things like "Somatic Recalibration Procedures" and "The Ethics of Embodied Consent."

I need Lulu's empanadas. I need a beer. I need Chipi on my chest and the sound of Paco's voice telling me I'm not crazy.

I walk out of 814 Ninth Street and the October air hits my face like a slap and I breathe it in because it smells like diesel and leaves and the city and it's mine, this air, this street, this life that I'm either about to save or completely lose.
