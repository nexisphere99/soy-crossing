## PASSAGE: t0_day01_wake [day-start]

Chipi's paw lands on my left eyelid at 6:14 AM. One minute before the alarm. She's done this every morning for three years and she has never once been late.

I shove her off gently and she drops to the pillow with the offended grace of a small god. The alarm goes off. I kill it. Lie there for ten seconds staring at the ceiling where the plaster has that crack shaped like the Baja Peninsula, the one I've been meaning to report to the super since February. February was three lifetimes ago.

Twenty-seven days.

The number sits in my chest like a stone I swallowed in my sleep.

I swing my legs off the bed. Feet on cold hardwood. The apartment is dim, blinds still down, October light pushing grey fingers through the slats. Smells like nothing because I forgot to set the coffee timer again, which means manual pour-over, which means I'm already five minutes behind a schedule I don't actually have but that my anxiety has built for me anyway.

Kitchen. Chipi beats me there. She sits on the counter next to the coffee grinder and stares at me with the specific contempt of an animal whose breakfast is thirty seconds late.

"Tranquila," I say. She is not tranquila.

I feed her first. Wet food, the salmon kind that costs $1.89 a can and smells like a fish market in July but makes her purr so hard she vibrates the floor tiles. Then coffee. Grind, filter, kettle, pour. The routine of it is the closest thing I have to meditation. Water spiraling over grounds. Steam rising. The smell filling the kitchen like something alive.

I drink it standing at the counter in boxer shorts and a t-shirt I've slept in for two nights. Eat toast over the sink. Butter, no jam, because the jam ran out on Saturday and going to the store for jam felt like too much commitment for a man whose checking account has $2,100 and whose rent is $1,400 and whose entire existence in this country has a best-before date stamped on it in bureaucratic ink.

The toast is dry. The coffee is good. Chipi finishes her salmon and jumps onto my lap, except I'm standing, so she jumps onto the counter and headbutts my elbow instead. I scratch behind her ears. She closes her eyes. We stand there for a minute in the grey morning light and this is fine. This exact moment is fine.

Then I look at my phone. 6:38 AM. Three emails from job boards I'm still subscribed to. A LinkedIn notification from a recruiter who definitely hasn't read my profileand a calendar reminder I set last night:

**8:30 AM / Shape of You Inc. / 814 Ninth Street. Don't be late.**

My stomach drops.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_mirror

The shower is hot. I stand under it longer than I should because the water bill is included in rent and this is the only luxury I haven't had to cut. Steam fills the bathroom. Chipi sits on the toilet lid and watches me through the glass door like a surveillance operative who's seen some things and isn't impressed.

I get out. Towel around waist. Wipe the mirror with my palm and there I am.

Same face. Twenty-five years of it. Brown skin, dark hair that needs a cut. Really needs a cut; it's curling over my ears and the back touches my collar and I look like a telenovela extra who's been through something. Dark eyes with circles under them that no amount of sleep seems to fix. Jaw that my mother says is my father's jaw, strong, she says, but I just see the shadow on it from not shaving since Thursday.

I'm not bad-looking. I know that the way you know facts about yourself, distantly, without much use for the information. Lyla calls me "hot stuff" but Lyla also calls her laptop "hot stuff" when it crashes, so the bar is on the ground.

I get dressed. Dark jeans, clean. The navy blue henley because it's the nicest thing I own that doesn't require ironing. Boots. I look like a developer who might also fix your sink. Which is approximately what I am, minus the development job and the functioning sink.

I look at myself one more time. In a few hours someone is going to explain how to put this brain, this specific consciousness, these memories, this voice, into a body that doesn't look anything like the one in this mirror. A woman's bodyand I'm going to sit there and take notes on my phone and nod like it's a Tuesday standup at Brevity Labs, because I need a visa and this is the only door that hasn't closed in my face.

"Okay," I say to my reflection. My reflection says nothing helpful back.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_lulu_morning

Lulu's bodega is four blocks from my apartment, on the corner where Ninth and Montclair meet. The bell above the door has been broken since 2019 but she hung a string of Christmas bells on the handle instead, so every customer sounds like a tiny sleigh arriving.

"¡Mijo!" Lulu's behind the counter in her blue apron, rosary beads swinging from the register, a newspaper spread in front of her that she's been reading with a magnifying glass because she refuses to get new prescription lenses. "You're up early for a man with no job."

"I have a job. Starting today."

"¿De verdad?" She puts down the magnifying glass. Touches her crucifix. "What kind of job?"

"Research."

"Research." She says the word like she's tasting something unfamiliar. "Like a scientist?"

"Something like that."

"Mijo, you're a computer boy. Since when do you do science?"

"Since last week."

She looks at me for a long moment. Lulu is fifty-five years old and has run this bodega for twenty-two of those years and she has a radar for bullshit that would put the TSA to shame. Her eyes narrow. Then she reaches under the counter and pulls out a wax-paper bag.

"Empanadas. Still warm. You look like you need feeding before you go do your science."

"Lulu, I can't."

"On the house. First day." She pushes the bag across the counter. Beef and cheese. The smell alone is worth the walk. "And get a haircut, mijo. You look like a poet. Nobody trusts a poet."

I take the empanadas. She touches my hand on the bag, quick, warm, and says: "God watches, Manuel. You'll be fine."

I eat one empanada on the walk to the bus stop. It's perfect. Greasy and salty and the cheese pulls in long strings and burns the roof of my mouth and I don't care.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_bus

The 34 bus arrives three minutes late, which in this city is practically early. I climb on, tap my card, find a seat near the middle. The bus smells like diesel and someone's breakfast sandwich and the specific metallic funk of a vehicle that's been circulating since 5 AM.

My leg bounces. I press my knee down with my palm. It bounces again.

Outside, the city slides past. Dry cleaners, nail salons, a Dominican barbershop where three old men are already sitting outside in folding chairs despite the fact that it's forty-eight degrees. A woman jogs past the bus at the light, ponytail swinging, earbuds in, breath clouding in the October cold. She's wearing running tights and a sports bra under an unzipped jacket and I notice, the way I always notice, and then I think about the fact that in a few hours someone is going to explain how a body like that works from the inside and my leg starts bouncing again.

The guy next to me is reading a book. I can see the cover from the corner of my eye. Something about habits. Seven habits. Twelve habits. However many habits it takes to not be falling apart on a municipal bus at 8:15 in the morning. He turns a page. He looks calm. I hate him a little.

My phone buzzes.

Mamá. A voice note. I don't play it on the bus, not with strangers around, but I know what it says because she sends one every Monday morning. It'll be a prayer, followed by gossip about Tía Rosa, followed by "¿y cuándo me presentas a una novia?" When are you going to introduce me to a girlfriend. The same question since I was twenty-two. The answer hasn't changed. The shame about the answer hasn't either.

The bus turns onto Ninth Street. My stop is four blocks away. I can see the numbers on the buildings counting up. 802. 806. 810.

814.

The building is nondescript. Five stories. Brick and glass. There's no sign. No logo on the door. Just a number: 814and a small camera above the entrance that tracks me as the bus rolls past.

I pull the cord. The bus stops. I stand up.

Twenty-seven days. $2,100 in checking. A family in Guadalajara praying for good newsand a door that might lead somewhere or nowhere, but I can't afford nowhere, so here I am.

I step off the bus. The October air bites through my henley. My breath makes a small cloud.

"Vámonos," I say, to nobody. Let's go.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_evening_bodega

The sky is the color of a bruise when I come out of 814 Ninth Street. Purple and yellow at the edges, going dark. October evenings arrive sudden and non-negotiable.

My head is full. Overfull. Transfer methods. Clone designations. The 72-hour window. Elena Cordero's voice cutting through the information like a scalpel through gauze, clean and deliberate and slightly terrifyingand Rafael's ninety minutes of questions, peeling me open layer by layer like he was looking for a specific organ and he'd know it when he found it.

I need food. I need beer. I need Chipi on my chest. But first I need to walk past something normal, so I take the long way home and stop at Lulu's.

The bells on the door. She looks up from her newspaper, different section now; she's made progress through the day.

"Ay, mijo, you look like you've seen a ghost."

"Long day."

"Was the science good?"

"The science was... a lot."

She studies me the way she studies the newspaper, like she's going to find meaning if she looks hard enough. Then she puts down her magnifying glass and starts filling a bag without being asked. Rice. Beans. Two Modelosand the empanadas, different ones from this morning, chicken and green chile.

"How much?"

"Twelve dollarsand you eat all of it, you hear me? You're too skinny. Your mother would be ashamed."

"My mother is four thousand miles away."

"God is closer." She touches her crucifix. "And God agrees with me. Eat."

I pay. She pats my hand on the counter again, that quick warm pressure. I leave with the bag against my chest, the warmth of the empanadas bleeding through the paper and into my ribs.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_evening_home

Chipi is at the door. Not near the door. At the door. Sitting in the exact center of the doormat, staring at the doorknob like she's been counting the seconds since I left and has opinions about every single one.

"Hola, gatita."

She meows. Not the hungry meow. The offended meow. The "I have been alone for eleven hours and I want you to know that I noticed" meow.

I drop my bag on the counter. Feed her the expensive salmon. She forgives me in approximately four seconds, which is her usual grace period. The sound of her eating fills the kitchen, tiny wet crunching that would be disgusting from any other creature but from Chipi is somehow the most comforting sound in my apartment.

I crack a Modelo. Pour it into a glass because I'm not an animal. I am a man who stands at his kitchen counter in a navy henley drinking beer from a glass and eating empanadas while his cat eats fish on the floor and the apartment is quiet. Not peaceful quiet.

The kind of quiet where the walls hold the day's noise and play it back in fragments. Elena's voice: "The body is a vessel. Consciousness is the pilot. The vessel has its own momentum." Rafael's pen on his clipboard. The scratch of it. The pauses where he waited and I squirmed and he watched me squirm with the patience of a man who's seen a thousand people squirm and finds each one individually interesting.

I eat the empanadas standing up. Chicken and green chile. Lulu doesn't mess around. The heat sits on my tongue and then slides down my throat and settles in my stomach next to the beer and the anxiety.

My phone is on the counter. I should call Paco. I should. He's the one who sent me to that door. He deserves to know what was on the other side.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_evening_cook

Rice and beans. My mother's recipe, or the approximation I've reverse-engineered from her voice notes over four years. She doesn't measure anything. "Un poquito de esto, mijo." A little of this. A little of that. Three cloves of garlic but small ones, or two but big. Cumin until it smells right. She cooks by feel and I cook by trying to feel what she feels from four thousand miles away.

The rice goes in the pot. I add water, too much, I always add too much, the rice will be soft instead of fluffy and my mother would know instantly and forgive me instantly and tell me to add more garlic next time.

While the rice cooks I sit at the kitchen table. It's a two-person table from IKEA that I assembled drunk by myself on my first night in this apartment. One leg is slightly shorter than the others. I've had a folded napkin under it for three years. The second chair has never been used by anyone who wasn't Chipi.

Chipi jumps up now. Sits in the second chair. Looks at me across the table like we're about to have a conversation she's been putting off.

"I'm going to be transferring my consciousness into women's bodies for money," I tell her. "Thoughts?"

She blinks slowly.

"Yeah. Me too."

The rice timer goes off. I make a plate. Sit down. Eat at the table because sitting is supposed to be better for digestion or mental health or both, and I could use help with both.

The food is okay. Rice is too soft. Beans are good. I eat all of it because Lulu told me to and because I'm hungry in a way that isn't just about food. I'm hungry for something to feel solid, and a full stomach is close enough.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_evening_phone

Three messages while I was eating.

**James (9:02 PM):** "Dinner this week? I found a new Thai place. My treat."

His treat. Always his treat. James survived the Brevity Labs cuts and I didn't and he handles the guilt of that by feeding me like a rescue animal every two weeks. I should hate it. I don't. His pad thai recommendations are flawless and guilt-funded pad thai still tastes like pad thai.

I reply: **"Thursday?"**

Three dots. Then: **"Done. Hope the new job is good, man. You deserve good things."**

I stare at that text for a while. "You deserve good things." James says stuff like that. Just drops it into a conversation like it's obvious, like he doesn't understand that some people hear that sentence and their throat closes up because they're not sure it's true and they're definitely not sure someone should be saying it to them on a Monday night when they're standing in their kitchen with rice on their shirt.

**Mamá (voice note, 2:14 PM):** I play it now, low volume, phone against my ear.

"Mijo, I went to church this morning and I lit a candle for you. Tía Rosa was there, she says hello, she looks terrible, she's gained weight since the thing with her gallbladder but don't tell her I said that. Your father is fine, he's fixing the neighbor's truck again, I told him to charge more but he never listens. I made pozole yesterday and I thought of you, I'll send you the recipe but you won't make it because you never make what I send you. I love you, mijo. Call me backand when are you going to find a nice girl? You're twenty-five. When I was twenty-five I already had your brother."

I listen to it twice. Her voice fills the kitchen like a presence. Warm and relentless and absolutely sure of things I'm not sure of at all.

I don't call back. Not tonight. I can't say "research, biometric" to my mother. She'd see through it in three words and then I'd have to explain and the explaining would unravel me. Tomorrow. I'll call her tomorrow.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_paco_call

Paco picks up on the first ring.

"¡HERMANO! ¿Fuiste? Tell me everything. Did they scan your brain? Is it a cult? Is the building weird?"

"Paco."

"Are there, like, pods? Tell me there are pods."

"Paco. Breathe."

He breathes. Audibly. I can hear him adjusting, probably sitting up from whatever couch he was melted into. There's music in the background, reggaeton, because Paco's apartment is a permanent reggaeton zone.

"Okay. I'm breathing. Talk to me."

I sit on the couch. Chipi immediately materializes on my lap, because she has a sixth sense for phone calls and believes every conversation requires her presence. I rub her head. She purrs.

"I went. The building is normal. Regular building. There's a receptionist named Yuki who looks like she could process the apocalypse without losing her place in a spreadsheet."

"Hot?"

"Not the point."

"Never the point, always the question."

"There's an instructor, Elena. Former neurosurgeon. She's..." I search for the word. "Intense. She explained the science. All of it. How they map your consciousness. The transfer methods. The different body types."

"And the bodies are, what, women?"

"Mostly. The high-paying work is male-to-female transfer."

Long pause. Paco goes quiet and when Paco goes quiet you pay attention because the man generates noise the way the sun generates light, constantly, involuntarily, and you notice the absence immediately.

"Hermano."

"Yeah."

"So you're really doing this. Body swapping."

"I'm really doing this."

"And you're going to be in a woman's body."

"That's the job."

"Walking around with..."

"Paco."

"Tits, Manuel. I'm talking about titsand the other stuff. The whole situation."

"Yes. The whole situation."

Another pause. Shorter. Then: "This is either the wildest thing you've ever done or the smartest."

"Can it be both?"

"In my experience, the wildest things usually are." He laughs, that big Paco laugh that comes from somewhere near his navel. "Okay. How do you feel?"

I look at the ceiling. Chipi's purr vibrates through my thighs into the couch cushions.

"Like I ate too much information and not enough food. Lulu's empanadas are holding me together."

"Lulu's empanadas could hold the world together." He pauses. "And the visa thing? The sponsorship?"

"If I pass the training. If they hire me. Then they file the petition. Then I stay."

"How long's the training?"

"Six working days. Then an assessment."

"And if you fail?"

"Then I have about three weeks to find someone else. Or go home."

"You're not going home, Manuel."

"No."

"You're not going home because I didn't give you that address so you could go home. You're going to do this weird sci-fi body thing and you're going to pass their test and you're going to get your visa and then you're going to buy me dinner for once in your life."

"Dinner at Danny's. My treat."

"See? Already manifesting." He turns the music down. "Seriously though. Are you okay?"

I think about it. Really think about it. The answer is complicated and I don't want complicated right now.

"I'll tell you more when there's more to tell."

"Fair enough. Get some sleep, hermano. Big week."

"Night, Paco."

"Nightand Manuel?"

"Yeah?"

"Don't let them scan anything weird. I don't trust companies with my brain data."

"You don't trust companies with your email."

"EXACTLY. Buenas noches."

He hangs up. The silence rushes back in. I sit on the couch with Chipi and the Modelo and the quiet apartment and the twenty-seven days left on a clock I can't see but can always feel, ticking in the space between my ribs.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_evening_couch

Second Modelo. I drink it slower. Chipi stretches across my lap, all four legs extended, purring like a small engine. The TV is off. I don't turn it on. The quiet has a different quality tonight. Not the hostile quiet of the last few weeks, the "you're alone and everything is falling apart" quiet. This is more like a pause. The held breath between one thing ending and another beginning.

I think about Elena's lecture. The three transfer methods: pod, headset, sleep capsule. The way she described the sensation of transfer. "Thermal shift, perceptual compression, expansion." Like being pushed through a needle's eye and coming out the other side larger. Or at least different.

I think about Rafael's questions. The way he asked about dating. "Single. Four years." He wrote something down and I wanted to see what he wrote because I've been narrating my own loneliness for so long that someone else's version of it felt like it might be more accurate. Or at least more concise.

I think about tomorrow. Module 1 continues. The first transfer is Day 2. Tomorrow, someone is going to put my brain into a woman's body and I'm going to walk around in it and feel things I've never felt and then come back and be expected to debrief about it with a Brazilian ex-military psychologist who can read me like a receipt.

The beer is empty. Chipi is asleep. My phone screen says 11:17 PM.

"Tomorrow," I say to the dark apartment.

Nobody answers. That's fine. I'm used to that.

## PASSAGE: t0_day01_sleep

Teeth. Face. Lights off.

The bed. Queen-size, bought on sale at a furniture outlet three and a half years ago because it was cheap and I was optimistic and I thought maybe, at some point, someone would be in it besides me and a cat. That hasn't happened. The bed is too big. It's always too big. I sleep on the left side because the right side has Chipi and a pillow I don't use and the ghost of a person I haven't met yet.

Chipi settles against my hip. Her warmth is small and specific. I put my hand on her back. She purrs. The vibration moves through my hand into my wrist and up my arm and I feel it in my chest, this tiny engine of aliveness pressed against my body in the dark.

I close my eyes. The day replays in fragments.

Yuki: "Room 4. Don't be late."

Elena: "The body is a vessel. Consciousness is the pilot."

Rafael: "How long have you been single?" Four years. "Dating?" Hard to date when your residence is conditional.

Lulu: "God watches, Manuel."

Paco: "You're not going home."

James: "You deserve good things."

The fragments blur. My breathing slows. Chipi's purr gets quieter or I get further away from it, I'm not sure which.

Twenty-seven days. The bed is too big. Tomorrow they're going to teach me more about what it means to be inside a body that isn't mine. Tonight I'm lying in my own body in my own bed in an apartment I might not have in a month, and the last thing I hear before sleep takes me is Chipi's purr and the radiator clicking on and the distant sound of a bus on Ninth Street going somewhere I'm not.
