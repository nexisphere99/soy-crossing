## PASSAGE: t0_day03_lunch_cafeteria

The cafeteria at Shape of You is a room that wants to be a break room and almost makes it. Vending machines along one wall, a microwave that smells permanently of burned popcorn, four round tables with plastic chairs, a counter with a coffee machine that produces something technically classified as coffee. The fluorescents buzz at a frequency designed to remind you that relaxation is temporary.

I carry my tray to the table nearest the window. The window overlooks an alley and a dumpster, which is not inspiring, but the light is real and after two hours in transfer rooms with controlled environments, real light feels like a gift.

Turkey sandwich. Apple. Water. I eat mechanically. My hands feel too big for the sandwich. An hour ago, in Katya's body, my fingers were longer, thinner, the grip different. I'm holding a turkey sandwich with hands that feel borrowed from a different species and the bread keeps slipping.

Tommy drops into the chair across from me. His tray has three sandwiches and two apples and a chocolate milk. He eats like a man who was once told food might run out and decided never to test the theory.

"The mirror," he says, mouth half-full.

"Yeah."

"How long?"

"Four. Elena told me."

"Seven." He tears into the second sandwich. "She looked at me like I'd committed a crime. Seven seconds. I just stood there. The face in the mirror wasn't mine, obviously, but it was making my face, you know? Like, the expression was mine but the canvas was wrong and my brain just," he makes a sound like a computer powering down, "brrrrr."

"What was yours like? The clone."

"Same. Katya. Auburn hair, curves, the whole thing. I think we all got Katya today."

"The blush."

"Mierda, yes. The blush." He puts the sandwich down. "I'm standing there and the room is a little cold and the body just, my face, her face, started getting warm. Pink. I could feel the blood doing it. Like watching paint dry except the paint was inside me and I didn't have a brush."

"The nipple thing."

Tommy stares at me. "Bro."

"The temperature response. Elena mentioned it in the briefing."

"Yeah. Yeah, I know what you mean. Just, hearing you say 'the nipple thing' in a cafeteria with fluorescent lighting and a turkey sandwich is a specific experience I was not prepared for."

A tray slams down on the table. Georgia. She drops into the chair between us without invitation, the way she does everything, like an entrance fee was paid somewhere and we just missed the transaction.

"Boys." She grins. Wide. Dangerous. "How are we feeling about our bodies today?"

Tommy puts his face in his hands.

"That good, huh." She picks up her apple. Bites it. Chews while looking between us. "I had a dick for two hours yesterday. Two hours, gentlemen. You know what I learned?"

Tommy: "Do I want to know?"

"You absolutely do. I learned that erections have the emotional range of a smoke detector. They go off for everything. Cold air. Warm air. Thinking about food. Not thinking about anything. The body just decides 'hey, you know what this moment needs? A boner.' No context. No warning."

Tommy chokes on his chocolate milk. I laugh. Full, surprised, involuntary. The first time I've laughed inside this building. The sound comes out of my own throat in my own voice and it feels like cracking a window open in a room that's been sealed for days.

Georgia points her apple at me. "There it is. I've been watching you for three days, Manuel. That's the first time your face has done something besides 'stoic concern.' I was starting to think it was structural."

"My face does other things."

"Name one."

"Confusion. I do confusion well."

"Fair. Tommy?"

Tommy wipes chocolate milk from his chin. "I do panic. I'm told it's very expressive."

Georgia leans back. She's compact, muscular, mid-twenties, Black, with close-cropped hair and eyebrows that do more acting than most people's entire faces. She was a firefighter in Queens before SoY. She told us on Day 1 and then didn't explain why she left and nobody asked because Georgia has the kind of energy that suggests certain questions will be answered when she's ready and not before.

"The mirror got me too, by the way," she says, quieter. "Different reasons. I looked in the mirror and saw a man's body and my first thought was 'huh, this is what they see.' Not what I see. What men see when they look in the mirror. The posture. The shoulders. The way the body fills space without apologizing for it."

"You apologize for filling space?" Tommy.

"Not me personally. But the female body does. The default posture is, smaller. Legs together. Arms close. Make room. The male body doesn't do that. I stood in that mirror and the body was just, there. Taking up exactly the space it took up. No negotiation."

We sit with that. The cafeteria buzzes. Someone heats soup in the microwave and the smell is aggressively tomato.

"So we all got the mirror," I say.

"We all got the mirror."

Tommy finishes his third sandwich. "Did either of you get the knock?"

Georgia: "Male voice outside the door while I was half-dressed. As a man."

"Different test for you?"

"Different. The voice was female. Someone calling the male clone's name. Asking if he was coming to dinner and the body, the male body, the response was... confidence. Not warmth, not welcome. Just, she can wait. He's busy. The default was 'my time is more important than her concern.' I almost didn't open the door because the body didn't want to bother."

She looks at her apple. Takes another bite.

"I opened it. Partially dressed. The male body didn't care about being seen. Zero modesty reflex. The female voice outside could see my chest, my stomach, and the body's response was 'so what.' That was the lesson. The asymmetry. My body, my real body, the door stays closed until I'm covered. The male body couldn't even understand why the door would stay closed."

Tommy: "Mierda."

"Yeah." Georgia tosses the apple core into the trash from her chair. Perfect arc. Nothing but bin. "Mierda indeed."

Brett walks past without sitting down. Tray, sandwich, water, straight to the far table. He sits alone with his back to us.

Georgia watches him go. "He froze in the knock scenario. I heard Elena on the intercom. Eight seconds of nothing. Just stood there."

"Is he going to make it?" Tommy.

"Not my call." Georgia stands. "But if he does, it won't be because he figured out what the body was doing. It'll be because he figured out what he was doing."

She carries her tray to the return window. Pauses. Looks back at us.

"You two are alright. For straight men in a body-swapping program, you're alright."

She leaves. Tommy and I sit in the cafeteria with our empty trays and the ghost of her insight and the fluorescent buzz.

"Are we friends now?" Tommy says. "Is this what's happening?"

"I think so."

"Good." He crushes his chocolate milk carton. "I could use one of those."

## PASSAGE: t0_day03_lulu_bodega [Optional evening stop]

I detour to Lulu's on the way to Vega. I need milk for tomorrow's coffee and an empanada for tonight's whatever.

The bodega smells like Fabuloso and frying oil and the specific combination of packaged snacks and fresh produce that exists only in bodegas. The aisles are narrow. The ceiling is low. Every surface holds something, cans stacked on cans, bags of chips hung from clip strips, a rotating display of sunglasses that has been rotating since 2014.

Lulu is behind the counter. Lourdes Medina. Fifty-five. The kind of Mexican-American woman who calls everyone under forty "mijo" and everyone over sixty "compadre" and the rest of the world "trouble." She's wearing her reading glasses on a chain around her neck and her crucifix over them and her apron over everything. Her hands smell like cumin. They always smell like cumin.

"Mijo!" She sees me and the entire bodega gets smaller because Lulu's attention fills rooms. "You look terrible."

"Thanks, Lulu."

"No, I mean it. The circles under your eyes. The hair." She gestures at my head like it's offended her personally. "When was the last time you got a cut?"

"I'm growing it out."

"Growing it out means a plan. That is not a plan. That is neglect." She reaches under the counter and produces a foil-wrapped package. "Empanada. Beef. Fresh. You need it more than my other customers and that includes Señor Peralta who has diabetes and no judgment."

"How much?"

"Don't insult me." She pushes the empanada across the counter. "You eat. I pray. God handles the rest."

I grab milk from the cooler. Bring it to the register. She rings it up, $3.50, and then pauses with my change in her hand.

"Mijo. You're coming in here more."

"I like the empanadas."

"You like not being alone." She says it without judgment. Just observation. Like reporting the weather. "That's not a bad thing. But eventually you need a person, not a bodega. God makes girlfriends, mijo. Free of charge. You just have to show up."

"Lulu."

"I'm just saying." She hands me the change. "The girl at the ramen place, Danny's daughter, she's single."

"Danny doesn't have a daughter."

"Fine. The girl at the bar, then. The one with the tattoos. Paco told me about her."

"Paco told, how do you know Paco?"

"He comes in here. He buys plantain chips and talks. That man talks more than my sister during confession. He says there's a bartender you like."

I take the milk and the empanada. "I'm leaving."

"Friday! Bring her by! I'll make pozole!"

"Goodbye, Lulu."

"POZOLE, MANUEL."

I walk out. The bell over the door jingles. Behind me, I hear Lulu say to no one: "That boy. Ay."

## PASSAGE: t0_day03_vega_adriana_full [First meeting, extended]

[Note: The daily life section contains the initial Adriana meeting. This passage expands the bar scene with full dialogue, interaction depth, and atmosphere for the side quest "Adriana's Orbit" initiation.]

Two hours at Vega. The cumbia gives way to a salsa track, then a bachata, then something modern with a reggaeton beat that the three soccer guys at the corner table approve of by turning up their argument.

Between customers, Adriana and I trade words. Not a conversation, exactly. More like two people building a bridge from opposite banks, tossing planks across the water when neither of them is busy.

Second exchange, maybe forty-five minutes in. I'm on my second mezcal. She's cutting limes. The knife moves fast, practiced, the segments dropping into a plastic container with mechanical precision.

"So. Research guy."

"So. Bartender."

"Adriana. Not 'bartender.' I don't call you 'customer.'"

"You called me 'the quiet one.'"

"That was Paco's word. I call you Manuel. Now that I know your name." She finishes the limes. Washes the knife. "How long have you been in the city?"

"Four years."

"From?"

"Guadalajara."

"Mmm." She nods like she's filing it. "I'm Bronx. Born and raised. Never left except for a semester in nursing school that I quit because I decided I liked people better when they're drunk and honest."

"That's a specific career pivot."

"Most pivots are. Nobody wakes up and says 'I'm going to tend bar for seven years.' You fall into it. The falling turns into a career once you realize you're good at it and the money isn't terrible and the hours let you paint during the day."

"You paint?"

"I do things." She smiles. Doesn't elaborate. Moves to serve a couple who just sat down at the end of the bar.

Third exchange. Maybe ninety minutes in. The bar has thinned. The soccer guys left. The couple in the corner is kissing. The woman with the paperback is on her third glass of wine and her book is facedown on the bar, which means it's either very good and she's savoring it or very bad and she's given up.

Adriana returns. Leans on the bar. Her forearms are flat on the wood and the tattoo sleeves are fully visible now. I can trace the designs. The left arm is botanical, flowers and vines and thorns, intricate, almost Japanese in the precision of the linework. The right arm is geometric, mandalas and sacred geometry patterns interspersed with skulls, small ones, tucked into the negative space like Easter eggs.

"Can I ask you something?" she says.

"You just did."

"Comedian. Here's the real question." She tilts her head. Studies me. "You come in here alone on a Wednesday night. You order mezcal, which is not a casual drink. You sit at the bar, not at a table. You don't check your phone. You don't watch the TV. You just sit and drink and think. What are you thinking about?"

I turn the glass. The mezcal catches the dim light, amber.

"Today I learned that consent is an architecture, not a feeling."

She blinks. Wasn't expecting that.

"Okay. That's not what people usually think about at bars."

"I'm not usual."

"Getting that." She pours herself a water. Drinks. "Consent is an architecture. What does that mean?"

"It means that saying yes isn't enough. You have to build the yes. Document it. Define it. Make it specific and time-limited and scope-limited so that everyone involved knows exactly what was agreed to and nobody can pretend they didn't."

She sets the water down. Looks at me differently. Not the bartender-customer look. Something with more weight behind it.

"That sounds like something a person learns the hard way."

"It's something I learned today. At work."

"Your mysterious biometric job teaches you about consent."

"Among other things."

"Manuel." She leans closer. Not far. Just enough that I can smell something, smoke and vanilla, light, not perfume exactly, more like the scent of a person who exists in a bar where mezcal smoke hangs in the air. "You're carrying the world's most interesting secret. I can see it. It's sitting right behind your eyes."

"If I told you, it wouldn't be a secret."

"Fair." She straightens. Pours herself a shot. Holds it up toward me.

"To secrets."

"To secrets."

We drink. She goes back to work. The paperback woman pays and leaves. The kissing couple separates long enough to order another round. A man walks in alone, sits at the end of the bar, orders a beer without making eye contact with anyone. Wednesday night in a small bar in a specific neighborhood in a city of millions.

I pay. She makes change. I leave a tip.

"Same time next week?" she says.

Something in my chest tightens. Not arousal. Not romance. Just, the recognition of being seen. In a week of being inside other people's bodies, someone looked at my body, my face, my specific human presence, and found it worth a second question.

"Maybe tomorrow."

"Tomorrow works too."

At ten-fifteen the bar empties enough that the music fills the gaps between people instead of competing with them. A bolero plays, the singer's voice husky and unhurried, and the bass line walks through the room like someone who knows where they're going but isn't in a rush to get there.

Adriana wipes down the bar in front of me. Not because it needs wiping. Because the motion gives her hands something to do while her eyes do the real work.

"You haven't checked your phone once."

"Should I?"

"Most people check every ten minutes. Reflex. Like blinking but with dopamine." She folds the rag. "You've been here two hours and your phone has stayed in your pocket. That's either discipline or you don't have anyone to check for."

The observation sits between us like a card dealt face-up.

"Bit of both," I say.

She nods. Doesn't push. That's the thing about Adriana, at least tonight, the first night. She pushes exactly once and then she waits. The waiting isn't passive. She's watching. Cataloguing. Reading me the way she reads every customer who sits at her bar, except she's reading me with a slightly different vocabulary, one that includes the word "interesting" and the willingness to spend time on it.

"Last call is at eleven," she says. "But you can stay until I close if you want. Midnight."

"I should go. Early morning."

"The biometric body research."

"That's the one."

She smiles. Not wide. Just the left corner of her mouth lifting, an asymmetrical smile that makes one dimple appear and gives her face a quality of knowing something you don't and being amused by the gap.

"Go home, Manuel. Big day tomorrow."

"How do you know it's a big day?"

"Every day is a big day for a man carrying a secret that interesting." She takes my glass. Washes it. Puts it upside down on the rack with a precision that mirrors the way she cut the limes, the way she pours, the way she does everything behind this bar, with the competence of someone who has turned a craft into an extension of her body.

I leave money on the bar. She looks at the tip and raises an eyebrow.

"Paco tips well. You tip better."

"I'm investing in being welcome."

"You're already welcome. The tip is just proving you were raised right."

I stand. Put on the olive green jacket. She watches me zip it, a casual observation, the kind of inventory a person takes when they're deciding whether someone will come back.

"Goodnight, Adriana."

"Goodnight, research guy."

I leave. The door closes behind me. The red neon V reflects off the wet sidewalk, it rained while I was inside, and the puddles hold the letter like a secret kept in water. I walk home in the cold with mezcal warm in my stomach and the echo of her voice calling me "research guy" instead of Manuel, which somehow feels more intimate, like a nickname she invented in real time and decided to keep.

The rain has stopped but the city smells like wet concrete and fallen leaves and somewhere, faintly, like someone's window is open, garlic frying in a pan. My boots leave prints on the wet sidewalk. My hands are in my pockets. My reflection appears in the window of a closed barbershop, and for one disorienting fraction of a second I expect to see auburn hair and pale skin. I see my own face. Dark hair. Brown skin. The shadow under the eyes. Mine.

I keep walking.
